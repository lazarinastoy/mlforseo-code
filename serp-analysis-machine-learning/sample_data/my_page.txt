## Identify your search competitors and categorise them based on intent with an LLM

In the process of competitor analysis, it's crucial to begin tagging the sites we observe or consider as our competitors, based on the type of site we are competing against. For instance, sites like Wikipedia and Investopedia are primarily wiki-based and have an informational intent. They don't aim to sell a product but serve as resources for explanatory content. Other examples include educational platforms like Coursera and Udemy, which offer courses and learning opportunities. There are also news websites, review platforms like G2 or TechTarget, and various technology or business competitors. This categorisation is important as it paints a clear picture of what is ranking for the topics and keywords being analysed.



This approach aids in understanding where the opportunities lie and how Google positions different types of websites. With ongoing updates to Google's search algorithm, a significant factor in Google's assessment of topical authority is how consistently a website focuses on its primary content or "stays in its lane."

This context emphasises why it's important to analyse SERPs by classifying competitors into their intent category using a classification algorithm or even building a custom taxonomy of websites and their intent labels. This can help you identify the real competition in the SERP, as some positions might be reserved for certain 'big players' in their niches, as Google still would like to surface a diverse set of results, particularly for ambiguous queries.

Here's how to get started:

- Tagging websites based on intent: In your competitor analysis, it's essential to tag websites based on their intent categories: informational, commercial (or transactional), or navigational. This provides a holistic view of intent.

- Understanding search intent analysis : It's important to note that while these categories are comprehensive, they are not exclusive. Most queries can be assigned to one dominant intent, but plenty carry mixed intent. Treat your labels as the dominant intent, not the only one.

These initial steps will allow you to plot your ranked websites in buckets, showing you which types of websites are given more visibility for your target keywords, and what their average position is.



3. Calculate share of search: Additionally, calculate the share of search per domain, per site category, or per intent category. This involves a straightforward calculation and is an integral part of understanding the competitive landscape.



In 2026 I calculate it two ways, and I'd suggest you do the same:

- Share of ranking slots: the percentage of all the top-10 positions a domain, site type or intent holds. Simple, and free of assumptions.

- Click-weighted share: each slot weighted by an estimated click-through rate for its position, so a position 1 counts for far more than a position 9. The CTR curve is an assumption, so use your own Search Console data or a CTR study you trust, and say which one you used.

## How I'd run the categorisation today

The principle hasn't changed: you define the categories, the model applies them. What has changed is how reliable you can make that.

- Start with a rule-based lookup. You already know a lot of your competitors. Put them in a simple domain-to-category list (Wikipedia is a wiki, Reddit is a forum, .edu is learning) and match URL patterns like /best- or /vs- for comparison pages. It is free, instant and explainable, and those known domains double as examples for the model.

- Send the model the domain and a couple of its ranking titles. "What does this site publish?" is far easier to answer from titles than from a bare domain name.

- Use structured outputs. Current models from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google can be constrained to a JSON schema. Put your categories in the schema as an allowed list, and the model physically cannot invent a new category or return a chatty paragraph instead of a label. This one change removes most of the clean-up I used to do by hand.

- Spot-check a sample by hand before you trust the totals, especially for the "other" bucket.

In the notebook, the schema part looks like this (it uses Claude, but any model with structured outputs works the same way):

schema = {"type": "object", "additionalProperties": False, "required": ["labels"],
          "properties": {"labels": {"type": "array", "items": {
              "type": "object", "additionalProperties": False,
              "required": ["domain", "category", "intent"],
              "properties": {"domain": {"type": "string"},
                             "category": {"type": "string", "enum": CATEGORIES},
                             "intent": {"type": "string", "enum": INTENTS}}}}}}

resp = client.messages.create(
    model=LLM_MODEL, max_tokens=4000,
    output_config={"effort": "low", "format": {"type": "json_schema", "schema": schema}},
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}])

A word of caution, because I'm a broken record on this: a general-purpose LLM is great for custom categories that no off-the-shelf classifier knows about. For standard tasks with a purpose-built API (entities, sentiment), use the purpose-built API. Which brings us neatly to the next two methods. ## Identify prominent entities from ranking web results with Google's Natural Language API

Entity extraction using Google's Natural Language API  is an underutilised but highly effective tool in SEO. This process can be applied in SERP analysis to analyse titles and meta descriptions, and it's straightforward to set up.

Essentially what this analysis does is to demonstrate the most prominent entities that are mentioned throughout the titles and meta descriptions in the ranked results, which might indicate a content direction for your strategy. Of course, for a more detailed analysis, you can scrape the content from the pages , then analyse the content itself for entities.

If you want to know more ways to incorporate entity analysis in SEO, check out my guide to what entity extraction is and where it fits in SEO .

Here's how to get started:

Set up the prerequisites: You'll need an API key, which can be obtained by following Google's setup guide . Then use the entity analysis Google Sheets template , which, although not originally created by me, is based on code available in Google's documentation  for the Natural Language API. The step-by-step walkthrough is in entity extraction with Google's Natural Language API in Google Sheets . The first 5,000 units a month are free per feature (a unit is 1,000 characters), which covers a lot of titles and meta descriptions.

Input data: Enter URLs (or another ID column) and meta descriptions into the tool. The entities and sentiments are then extracted automatically.





Understanding the output:

- Entity: recognised thing or concept, based on Google's database and model training.

- Salience: the importance or relevance of the entity in the context of the snippet.

- Sentiment score and magnitude: these indicate the polarity and the strength of the expressed emotion.

- Number of mentions: this shows the prominence of the entity in the snippet.

One 2026 caveat on versions. Google released v2 of the Natural Language API in August 2023, running on a newer model. For this analysis, stick with v1: v2 dropped the salience score and the Wikipedia URL metadata, and it doesn't have the entity sentiment method at all (release notes ). The Sheets template and my notebook both use v1's analyzeEntitySentiment, so you get entities, salience and per-entity sentiment in one call. If you do use v2 for entities, the number of mentions is the closest stand-in for salience.

Visualisation: Analyse the most common entities mentioned in all meta descriptions for the SERPs collected. This requires careful keyword and topic research beforehand to ensure relevant and targeted analysis.







Data integration: Blend this data with other sources, like the data from your bulk SERP analysis, to get a comprehensive view.



This approach allows for a deeper understanding of the topics and keywords in your analysis, enabling more precise SEO strategies. It can also help you uncover entities that are not captured by popular keyword research tools like Semrush .

Prefer Python, or no API key yet? The notebook runs the same v1 call over every title and description and summarises each entity: how many results mention it, for how many keywords, its average position and salience, and whether it shows up in titles or only in descriptions. With no key, it falls back to the free spaCy library. Treat that fallback as a first look: on the sample data, the small spaCy model tagged "machine learning" as a person. Which is a pretty good illustration of why I prefer a purpose-built, knowledge-graph-backed API for this job, and why I compared the options in which NLP API to use for entity and sentiment analysis .

What's new in 2026. The notebook scores every title and description with v2's analyzeSentiment (sentiment is where the newer model is a clear upgrade, and v2 still returns both score and magnitude), then labels each one as positive, negative, neutral or mixed. ## Analyse the language structures using n-grams

In exploring language use analysis, consider the power of n-grams, a string-matching approach  that can reveal much about keyword combinations and their effectiveness. Even if coding isn't your forte, an LLM can be immensely helpful here. Back when I built this, I used a prompt in OpenAI's playground to create an Apps Script formula in JavaScript to identify the main n-grams (groups of words) in a text. The process required a few tries to refine the prompt, but the resulting function performed the intended analysis.

The original 2022 approach: a GPT-3-generated Apps Script function.

That first version split text into n-grams but didn't count them, lowercase them or strip punctuation, so I've replaced it with a tested custom function. Paste ngrams.gs from the code hub  into Extensions → Apps Script, save, and use it like any formula:

=NGRAMS(B2:B100, 2)            top bigrams in a column of titles
=NGRAMS(C2:C100, 3, 20, TRUE)  top 20 trigrams, stop words removed

It returns each n-gram, how many times it appears, and in how many titles, which is the number I care about most (one spammy title repeating a phrase five times shouldn't win).

The key here is to analyse bigrams (pairs of words) or trigrams (triplets) in titles or meta descriptions, uncovering the most prevalent keyword combinations. This analysis, especially when refined to exclude common stop words, can yield intriguing results.



For example, in my analysis of a specific set of keywords, the expression "what is" emerged as a frequent occurrence across titles. This insight can be pivotal in understanding the type of content Google favours for your chosen keywords. Extend this analysis to focus on specific aspects like commercial intent or product-related keywords, and you'll uncover the n-grams that dominate in these contexts.

Here is the nuance I'd add today: run it twice. "What is" is made entirely of stop words, so if you strip stop words first (as most tutorials tell you to), that insight disappears. Keep stop words to see title patterns ("what is", "how to", "vs", "best... 2026"), and remove them to see topics ("machine learning", "artificial intelligence"). On the 2026 sample data, "what is" still appears in 11 of 52 ranking titles (eight of them for "what is machine learning", the rest for "machine learning examples" and "machine learning vs ai"), every one an informational result, while the commercial results lean on "vs", "top" and "2026". Same method, three years later, same lesson.

Such analysis, simple yet profound, should be an integral part of your SERP analysis and can help you formulate the title and meta description  structures to generate as part of your content strategy.

## Frequently asked questionsWhat is SERP competitor analysis?
SERP competitor analysis means studying the pages that rank for your target keywords (who they are, what type of site they are, what intent they serve, what they talk about and how they phrase it) so you can see where the opportunities are and what Google rewards for those queries. Your search competitors are often not your business competitors: wikis, course platforms, forums and review sites all compete for the same positions.How can machine learning improve SERP analysis?
Machine learning lets you do at scale what would take days by hand: classify hundreds of ranking domains by site type and intent, extract the entities mentioned across every title and meta description, score their sentiment, and find the most common word patterns. You end up with a structured picture of the SERP you can measure and track, rather than a gut feel from scrolling through results.Can I do ML-enabled SERP analysis without coding?
Yes. Entity and sentiment analysis run in Google Sheets with free templates and a Google Natural Language API key, the n-gram analysis is a single =NGRAMS() custom function, and the Data Studio template handles the visualisation. The notebook is there for when your keyword lists get big or you want everything in one place.Should I use v1 or v2 of Google's Natural Language API for entity analysis?
For entity analysis, v1. Version 2 (released in August 2023) dropped the salience score and Wikipedia URL metadata, and it has no entity sentiment method. For document-level sentiment, v2's newer model is a good choice. Both share the same free tier: 5,000 units a month per feature, where a unit is 1,000 characters.How do you calculate share of search from SERP data?
The simplest version is share of ranking slots: count how many of the top-10 positions across your keyword set each domain (or site type, or intent) holds, and divide by the total. A click-weighted version multiplies each slot by an estimated click-through rate for its position before summing, so higher rankings count for more. Say which CTR curve you used, because it is an assumption.Is ChatGPT (or another LLM) good for classifying SERP competitors?
It is good for custom categories that no ready-made classifier knows, as long as you give it your category list, a few examples and some ranking titles per domain, and constrain it with structured outputs so it can only answer from your list. Spot-check the results. For standard tasks like entity extraction and sentiment, a purpose-built API is more consistent and gives you richer metadata.
